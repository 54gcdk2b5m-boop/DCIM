How does a data centre change the place around it?

Data centres are getting bigger, and more are being proposed near UK towns and villages. This page explains how big they are, the four kinds of impact they have and the choices that drive them. It ends with two tools: one for residents, one for councils.

Section 1 · Data centre size

How big is a data centre?

Data centres are sized in megawatts (MW): how much electricity their servers can draw, not how much land they cover. But more MW means more buildings, cooling plant and substations, so more land. Section 2 · Four kinds of impact

What a data centre does to a place

We group every effect into four categories. Each one asks a different question, and each is driven by different choices. Each card lists what could go wrong and what could go right. Every fact carries a confidence mark: UK UK data or an estimate built on it; US US studies or our own rough model, so treat it as a guide. The method page shows how the main numbers are worked out and where they come from.

💷 Economic

Who gains money and work, and who pays?

What could go wrong

  • Permanent jobs: 25–150 at US sites of any size; UK plans claim up to about one per MW. US
  • Construction: up to about 1,500 workers for 12–18 months. US
  • Home prices: up 2–5% across host counties. US
  • Electricity bills: projected up 8% nationally, up to 25% in crowded regions. US
  • Community funds: no UK minimum yet; onshore wind's proposed £5,000 per MW a year is the nearest benchmark. UK

What could go right

  • Business rates: Slough's 40-plus data centres reportedly pay over £30m a year; councils keep a share. press
  • Construction work: up to about 1,500 jobs for 12–18 months, and a chance for local apprenticeships if the council asks. US
  • Community fund: a Local Benefit Plan can pay for local projects every year the site runs. UK

⚡ Resource extraction

What does it take from the area?

What could go wrong

  • Electricity: a 100 MW site uses about a year, as much as homes. model
  • Water: about a year with evaporative cooling, with a closed loop. model
  • Land: about ha per MW, so ha for 100 MW. UK
  • Grid capacity: planned UK sites would add 5.7 GW by 2030, about three times today's stock, with new substations and lines. UK

What could go right

  • Waste heat: Meta's Odense data centre recovers about 100,000 MWh of heat a year, enough to warm about 7,000 homes. Denmark
  • Less water: closed-loop cooling cuts on-site water by about 97%. model

🌿 Environmental

What does it put into the air, land and climate?

What could go wrong

  • Carbon: a 100 MW site emits a year in but in . UK
  • Air quality: testing backup diesel generators releases about 12 t of NOx a year. US
  • Noise: generators run at 85–100 dB at source. US
  • Local heat: land surface about 2 °C warmer after AI sites open. preprint
  • Wildlife: England requires a 10% gain in habitat for major development. UK

What could go right

  • Brownfield reuse: building on old industrial land avoids losing fields and can clean up contaminated ground. UK
  • Habitat gain: the required 10% biodiversity gain can create new habitat locally. UK
  • Cleaner heat: waste heat piped to homes can replace gas boilers. Denmark

🏛️ Cultural

How does it change the look, history and feel of a place?

What could go wrong

  • Green belt: 9 of 90 UK projects are on green or grey belt; the harm has rarely stopped approval on appeal. UK
  • Heritage: Court Lane, Iver was refused partly over the setting of a listed farmhouse, then allowed on appeal. UK
  • Landscape and farmland: the Abbots Langley applicant rated its own landscape effect "moderate adverse". UK
  • Community trust: 9 of 90 UK projects were refused or went to appeal; US places have passed 321 moratoriums. UK US

What could go right

  • Local investment: a community fund can pay for heritage, halls and local events. UK
  • New public space: the approved Abbots Langley scheme includes a new country park. UK

What is one dot?

Each dot in the next charts is 5 GWh of electricity

One dot is enough electricity to power about UK homes for a whole year.

5 GWh is 5 million kWh, the unit on your own electricity bill. A number that big is hard to picture, so this figure shows what one dot's worth of electricity could do. The pictures are there to count: each one is labelled with what it stands for.

Use the buttons above the chart to switch between homes, an electric car, kettles, and a 100 MW data centre, which burns through one dot in about two days. Keep the homes in mind: we use them to size up a whole site next.

Assumes 2,700 kWh per home a year (typical UK), 3.5 miles per kWh for an electric car and 0.1 kWh to boil a full kettle.

6 · Jobs

Each dot is one proposed UK data centre that publishes both its size and its promised permanent jobs; hover for the details. The red trend curve bends over: a site ten times bigger brings only about four times the jobs. The dashed line shows what would happen if jobs kept pace with size, at the average of per MW.

These are developers' and councils' own claims. US studies find 25–150 permanent staff even at the largest sites, and Meta's 5 GW Hyperion campus in Louisiana plans 1,000 permanent jobs, 0.2 per MW. Construction is the real jobs peak: up to 1,500 workers for 12–18 months.
