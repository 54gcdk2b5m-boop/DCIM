How the levy is calculated

The levy calculator turns a few facts about a proposed data centre into a yearly payment to the community. Every step is ordinary arithmetic on published figures. This page shows each step, the formula, a worked example and how sure we are of each number.

Size (MW)→Electricity→Carbon→Carbon cost→Levy rate→Annual levy→Lifetime total

Confidence marks: ●●● measured published or official figure · ●●○ estimate our arithmetic on good data · ●○○ rough an assumption or borrowed evidence; treat as a guide.

Worked example. Change these and every number below updates. They start at an average UK project.

  1. Start from the site's power

    Developers quote size in megawatts (MW), the most electricity the site can draw. Some quote only the servers (IT load). We convert that to total site power by adding the overhead for cooling and power losses, measured as PUE.

    site MW = IT MW × PUE

    PUE defaults to 1.3, the figure the UK Data Centre Covenant asks new sites to meet; typical sites run 1.3–1.6. ●●○ estimate

  2. Work out the electricity it uses in a year

    No site runs flat out. The load factor is the share of its capacity it uses on average across the 8,760 hours of a year.

    electricity (MWh) = site MW × 8,760 × load factor

    Load factor 55%, from the IEA via the US Congressional Research Service; 40–80% is the plausible range. ●○○ rough

  3. Turn electricity into carbon

    Each region's grid emits a different amount of CO₂ per unit of electricity, depending on how much comes from wind, nuclear or gas.

    carbon (t CO₂) = electricity (MWh) × grid carbon (g per kWh) ÷ 1,000

    Grid carbon is the 12-month average for the region from the National Energy System Operator, Nov 2025 to Nov 2026 (CC BY 4.0). A large new load usually pulls in extra gas power, so the real figure is likely higher than this average. ●●○ estimate

  4. Put a price on the carbon

    The government values a tonne of CO₂ when it weighs the costs and benefits of policies. We use that value to show what the site's emissions cost society. It is not a bill anyone pays; it is a yardstick.

    carbon cost (£ a year) = carbon (t) × £264

    DESNZ carbon values for 2026: low £132, central £264, high £396 per tonne, in 2020 prices. ●●● measured

  5. Choose how to set the levy rate

    There is no legal formula for a data centre levy. The UK Data Centre Covenant asks large sites for a Local Benefit Plan "linked to the development's size", so the calculator offers three ways to set a rate per MW:

    A flat rate per MW

    levy = rate × site MW

    + Simple, and comparable with wind and solar farms.

    − Ignores how much harm this site does.

    Benchmarks: onshore wind's proposed minimum is £5,000 per MW a year; solar's is £700 (DESNZ, Nov 2026). ●●●

    A share of the carbon cost

    rate = carbon cost × share ÷ site MW

    + Grows with the harm: dirtier grids pay more.

    − Carbon harms everyone, not just the village, so it is a weak case for a local payment.

    A target per household

    rate = £ per household × households ÷ site MW

    + Easy for residents to understand.

    − Depends on how the benefit area is drawn.

  6. Work out the yearly levy and what each household gets

    annual levy = rate × site MW  ·  per household = annual levy ÷ households

    Funds are usually spent on local projects rather than paid to homes; the per-household figure is a way to judge size. ●●○ estimate

  7. Add it up over the agreement

    Levies are usually index-linked, so each year's payment rises. The total is the sum of every year, plus any one-off payments.

    lifetime = levy × (1 + rise)⁰ + levy × (1 + rise)¹ + … for every year, + one-off items

  8. Add one-off items where they apply

    • New substation: £530,000, and new overhead line: £200,000 per km, from government guidance on community funds for transmission (2025). ●●●
    • Biodiversity: if habitat must be bought as statutory credits, the cost is units × 2 credits × the price for that habitat tier (£42,000 to £650,000 per credit, Defra). We suggest asking for the gain to be delivered locally instead. ●●●

  9. Judge the whole deal, not just the levy

    The calculator's checklist scores ten things a strong agreement usually includes: a levy of at least £5,000 per MW, waste heat for local homes, local hiring, water limits, noise limits, public reporting, local biodiversity gain, brownfield land, new renewable power and residents helping to spend the fund. Seven or more is a strong deal. This is our checklist, not a legal test. ●○○ rough

What the calculation leaves out

  • Marginal carbon. We use average grid carbon; a big new load mostly adds gas power, so emissions are likely higher.
  • Embodied carbon. Building the site and making its servers emits carbon too. It is not counted.
  • Water at power stations. Only water used on site is counted; no UK figure for power-station water has been sourced.
  • Business rates. Data centres pay business rates, part of which councils keep. They are separate from the levy and not modelled.
  • What MW means. Developers quote IT load, grid connection or campus build-out. Check which, or the levy will be off.
  • Local harms. Noise, landscape and heritage harm are listed on the calculator but not priced. They are reasons to ask for more, not a formula.
